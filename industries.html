Trade by trade

What ESS actually does for each one.

The same database underneath. Different vocabulary, different inspection rules, different way the money is earned.

01

Elevator

Every unit gets a record: controller, machine room, the MCP tasks that are due and the callbacks written against it. Technicians open that history in the hoistway with no signal, capture the visit, and the office bills it the same day. Modernization runs as a project with its own costing, not as a long repair ticket.

  • MCP book and category test calendar
  • Callback history by car and by building
  • Modernization and new install job costing
02

Escalators & moving walks

Step chains, handrails and drive units are serial-numbered assets on the same contract structure your cars already use. Inspections repeat on a cadence, routes stack by building, and one dispatcher runs escalator and elevator work off a single board instead of two systems that never agree.

  • Unit-level asset and service history
  • One dispatch board shared with elevator crews
  • Recurring inspection scheduling and reminders
03

Overhead door & dock

Door and dock contractors carry the same shape of work: a service contract per site, equipment counted by opening, and a repair that turns into a quote while the technician is still on the dock. Hamburg Overhead Door runs its contracts, parts and invoicing here, and the door business fits without bending.

  • Contract and site equipment lists
  • Truck stock and parts against the work order
  • Quote from the field, invoice from the office
04

Fire & life safety

Inspections drive the year and the record has to hold up years later. Deficiencies found on an inspection become quoted work instead of a note on paper, contracts renew on schedule, and every visit leaves a document the property manager can pull from the Customer Portal without calling the office.

  • Scheduled inspection programs by building
  • Deficiency to quote to work order
  • Inspection records in the Customer Portal
Why it travels

Three things have to be true before ESS is the right system.

This is the test we apply to a trade before telling a contractor we are a fit. Where all three hold, the platform behaves the way it does for an elevator company. Where they do not, you are better off with something else.

Equipment with a serial numberThe work has to belong to a machine, not to an address. A car, an escalator truss, a dock door, a fire panel: something you can hang a controller record, a warranty and a service history on, and something a technician can open in the field and see the last visit against. If the job is only a street address, the record has nowhere to live and the second visit starts from nothing.
Inspections the code datesSomebody outside your company decides when the next visit has to happen. Category 1 and category 5 tests, annual and semi-annual device checks, whatever the authority having jurisdiction calls it in your trade. Those dates belong on the equipment record and on the calendar, because a missed one is a compliance problem before it is a scheduling problem.
Contracts measured in yearsThe revenue comes from an agreement that renews, with escalations, units moving on and off coverage, and work billed both inside and outside the contract. That is what makes contract-level profitability worth reading, and it is why a renewal conversation should start with numbers rather than with a feeling about the account.
